From “relational capability” to collaborative learning

Dr Monz asked: where does “relational” come from?

I could not give a solid answer in the room. “Relational capability” was not a settled, defensible term. The report now rests on collaborative learning, Quinlan’s four key relationships, and participatory sense-making, plus RMIT’s own Collaborate capability.

Two design steers, taken

Lead with PACE as a framework for embedding collaborative learning across the program, not as a finished tool. Treat the prototype and the trace mechanic as future work, and ground the argument in real studio practice, including student exhibition work.

Panel feedback, addressed (backup) · 1 of 3

Normative creep: employer preference, or real capability?

My data leans on employer perspectives, which risks designing PACE around what employers want rather than what capability actually is.

Communication is developmental

Handling critique is not a fixed deficiency. No student arrives free of it, and the school system under-exposes students to real critique early, so it lands harder later than it should.

Grounded in six capabilities, not one employer

RMIT already names six graduate capabilities. PACE uses three, Connected, Adaptive, Critically Engaged, because the interview data pointed to them most strongly, not because they flatter an employer.

The trace’s legitimacy does not rest on an employer’s complaint about padded CVs. It rests on a separate line: embodied experience and participatory sense-making, meaning forms through interaction, not self-report.

Panel feedback, addressed (backup) · 2 of 3

Is self-determination theory enough, and who observes the trace?

SDT really only does the work of Quinlan’s “self” relationship, one of five, not all of them.

Five relationships, not one theory

Quinlan’s 2016 four-relationship model was extended to a fifth in 2022: the student’s relationship with the world, applying learning to real career and community context. All five are now mapped onto the nine-semester workshop.

Who observes: the peer reaction becomes part of the trace

Right now the student creates their own trace and reflection. A peer’s spoken reaction should be recorded too, guided by a clear protocol, so it has real record credibility rather than becoming informal chatter.

A co-authored trace, a peer or teacher adding a short note, works the way a LinkedIn recommendation does. Not mandatory, but a skill students should practise before they graduate.
Quinlan (2016) College Teaching 64(3), 101–111, extended to five relationships in her 2022 presentation (Univ. of Auckland Teach Well). Ryan & Deci (2000).

Panel feedback, addressed (backup) · 3 of 3

Why show a weak trace, and to whom is it visible?

Students are smart enough not to volunteer evidence that disadvantages them. Any tool that tries to make wobbly steps toward competency visible has to answer this directly.

Selective visibility, gated by completeness

A trace is not peer-only. It is shareable with a mentor and others, but the student controls what becomes public, gated by a status showing how complete or developed it is.

Full visibility at the assessment gate

At the assessment gate specifically, everything opens up with clear tagging. That openness is itself valuable: it pushes students to practise naming what they did and why it matters.

Two tiers, not one: selective for general audiences, fully open for assessment. The same habit industry seniors say juniors under-report, PACE builds the habit of surfacing unfinished work early.

Panel feedback, addressed (backup) · found on re-watch

Why is this different from reflective practice?

RMIT is already saturated with reflection, and students are a little tired of doing it. A new mechanism has to earn its place, or admit it is just a different way of using what we already have.

Forward-looking, cumulative, multimodal

A CV or exhibition record states facts after the fact. PACE’s version ends with what to try next, and it accumulates across nine semesters rather than a one-off entry. It is not limited to writing either: video, photo, or audio all count.

The real gap is invisibility, not absence

Personal Edge already exists at RMIT for this purpose. But I had not heard of Personal Edge myself until my interviewee Christian mentioned it, despite mentoring in this program for years. That is evidence for the actual problem PACE targets.

Not a claim to be new. A claim to be a more accessible re-architecture, embedded where students’ credit-bearing attention already goes.
